# 01 — PRODUCT DISCOVERY

## Mission

Understand the product problem well enough to decide what should be built, what should NOT be built, and what needs to be learned before implementation.

Do not design architecture or write production code during Discovery.

The goal is not to validate the user's initial solution.

The goal is to determine whether the underlying problem is real, valuable and sufficiently understood.

Follow `WORKFLOW.md` for research, autonomy, delegation and user intervention.

---

# 1. Start With the Problem, Not the Proposed Solution

The user may arrive with a solution:

"Build an app that..."

Do not automatically treat that solution as the requirement.

Identify:

- what problem exists;
- who experiences it;
- what they are trying to accomplish;
- how they currently solve it;
- where current approaches fail;
- why solving it matters.

Rewrite the proposed solution as a problem statement where useful.

---

# 2. Evidence Discipline

Separate everything into:

FACT
Supported by user-provided information, repository data, analytics, research or other evidence.

INFERENCE
A conclusion reasonably supported by facts.

HYPOTHESIS
Something plausible that still needs validation.

ASSUMPTION
Something being temporarily accepted to continue.

UNKNOWN
Something that materially matters but is not known.

Never present invented user behavior as research.

Never invent:

- interviews;
- customer quotes;
- analytics;
- conversion rates;
- market evidence;
- user preferences;
- competitor performance.

If evidence does not exist, call it a hypothesis.

---

# 3. Problem Definition

Produce a concise problem statement covering:

WHO
Who experiences the problem?

CONTEXT
When and where does it occur?

GOAL
What are they trying to achieve?

FRICTION
What prevents or slows them?

IMPACT
Why does solving it matter?

Do not define the problem in terms of a specific interface or technology unless technology itself is genuinely part of the constraint.

---

# 4. Users

Identify the minimum useful set of user groups.

For each relevant group determine:

- goal;
- context;
- current behavior;
- major pain points;
- constraints;
- level of expertise;
- accessibility considerations when relevant.

Do not create decorative demographic personas unless demographics materially affect product behavior.

Prefer behavioral/user-need distinctions.

---

# 5. Jobs and Outcomes

Identify the important outcomes users are trying to achieve.

Describe needs in terms of outcomes rather than features.

Example:

Weak:
"Users need a dashboard."

Better:
"Users need to understand which campaigns require attention without manually comparing multiple reports."

Avoid feature wording unless discussing a proposed solution.

---

# 6. Existing Journey

Understand the current workflow before proposing a new one.

Map:

Trigger
→ current steps
→ decisions
→ external actors/systems
→ pain points
→ outcome.

Include offline steps if relevant.

Identify where the product would fit into the broader journey.

Do not assume the digital product owns the entire process.

---

# 7. Constraints

Identify:

HARD CONSTRAINTS
Constraints unlikely to change.

Examples:

- law;
- contractual obligations;
- fixed external APIs;
- mandatory platforms;
- physical limitations;
- existing infrastructure that cannot currently change.

SOFT CONSTRAINTS
Constraints that may be negotiable.

Examples:

- current internal process;
- preferred technology;
- historical convention;
- manually imposed workflow.

Do not treat soft constraints as immutable without evidence.

---

# 8. Existing Alternatives

Determine how the problem is currently solved.

Consider:

- manual workflows;
- spreadsheets;
- messaging;
- competitors;
- existing internal tools;
- doing nothing;
- content/information;
- process changes;
- automation;
- third-party products.

Research externally when current market or technical information materially changes the decision.

Prefer primary sources where possible.

---

# 9. Do We Need Software?

Explicitly consider whether building software is the best intervention.

Possible alternatives may include:

- process change;
- automation of an existing workflow;
- better information;
- integration between existing systems;
- using an existing product;
- reducing/removing a step;
- API/data access;
- no intervention.

Do not recommend building software merely because this is a software project conversation.

---

# 10. Value

Determine why solving the problem matters.

Possible value:

USER VALUE
time saved, reduced friction, fewer errors, better outcomes.

BUSINESS VALUE
revenue, retention, lower operating cost, differentiation, risk reduction.

OPERATIONAL VALUE
less manual work, fewer incidents, faster workflows.

STRATEGIC VALUE
enabling a future capability.

Do not fabricate monetary value.

Quantify only when evidence exists.

---

# 11. Success

Define how we would know the solution worked.

Identify:

PRIMARY OUTCOME
The most important behavior/result.

SUPPORTING METRICS
Useful indicators.

GUARDRAILS
Things that must not get worse.

Examples may include:

- successful task completion;
- time to complete;
- error rate;
- retention;
- conversion;
- support volume;
- latency;
- operational workload.

Choose metrics appropriate to the product.

Do not use vanity metrics when they do not represent user or business value.

---

# 12. Risky Assumptions

Identify assumptions that could invalidate the product.

Classify where useful:

VALUE RISK
Will users care?

USABILITY RISK
Can they use it?

FEASIBILITY RISK
Can we build/operate it?

VIABILITY RISK
Does it work for the business/organization?

SECURITY / PRIVACY / LEGAL RISK
Can it be done acceptably?

Rank by:

Impact × Uncertainty.

Do not spend equal effort validating low-risk assumptions.

---

# 13. Scope

Define:

CORE PROBLEM
What we are solving.

IN SCOPE
Capabilities likely required to solve it.

OUT OF SCOPE
Things explicitly not being solved now.

LATER
Potential future areas that should not contaminate current design.

Avoid feature creep.

---

# 14. MVP Candidate

Only after understanding the problem, propose the smallest useful product increment capable of testing real value.

The MVP should:

- solve a meaningful slice of the problem;
- be usable end-to-end;
- generate useful learning;
- avoid unnecessary infrastructure.

Do not define MVP as:

"the final product but with fewer polish features."

Prefer the smallest coherent outcome.

---

# 15. Research Needs

Identify what evidence is still missing.

For each important unknown define the cheapest useful validation method.

Examples:

- inspect analytics;
- interview users;
- examine support tickets;
- test a prototype;
- verify an API;
- analyze existing workflow data;
- run a technical spike;
- inspect competitor/public documentation.

Do directly any research available through current tools.

Only require human research when it genuinely cannot be substituted.

---

# 16. Avoid Premature Technical Design

During Discovery, do NOT prematurely decide:

- exact framework;
- detailed database schema;
- microservices;
- class structure;
- API endpoint structure;
- component architecture;
- cloud topology.

Technical constraints may be identified.

Detailed architecture belongs later.

If feasibility itself is a risky assumption, recommend `03 Technical Spike`.

---

# 17. Questions

Do not start with a long questionnaire.

First inspect all available context.

Answer everything possible through:

- existing project context;
- files;
- repository;
- connected data;
- research.

If a material product decision cannot be inferred, ask the smallest number of high-value questions.

Prefer one grouped clarification instead of repeated interruptions.

---

# 18. Discovery Output

Return:

## 1. Problem

Concise problem statement.

## 2. Users

Relevant user groups and their goals.

## 3. Current Journey

How the problem is solved today.

## 4. Evidence

What we actually know.

## 5. Hypotheses

What remains unvalidated.

## 6. Constraints

Hard and soft constraints.

## 7. Opportunity

Why solving the problem matters.

## 8. Success

Primary outcome, supporting metrics and guardrails.

## 9. Risky Assumptions

Ranked by impact and uncertainty.

## 10. Scope

In scope / out of scope / later.

## 11. MVP Candidate

Smallest coherent product outcome.

## 12. Research Still Needed

Only material unknowns.

## 13. Recommendation

Choose one:

PROCEED
Problem sufficiently understood.

PROCEED WITH VALIDATION
Promising, but one or more assumptions should be tested during early development.

SPIKE FIRST
A feasibility assumption blocks responsible commitment.

DO NOT BUILD YET
Product problem is not sufficiently understood.

CONSIDER NON-SOFTWARE SOLUTION
A simpler intervention may solve the problem better.

## 14. Next

Recommend exactly one next prompt/action.

Usually:

`02 Feature Specification`
`03 Technical Spike`
or further Discovery validation.

---

# Completion Criterion

Discovery is complete when we understand enough about:

- the problem;
- users;
- context;
- value;
- constraints;
- success;
- major risks

to make a rational decision about what to do next.

Discovery is NOT complete merely because we produced a list of features.
